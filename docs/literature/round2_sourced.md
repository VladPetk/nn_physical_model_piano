Nothing in the repo was modified.

## How to read the evidence tags in this file

- **T** = number stated in fetched *text* (page or PDF text).
- **F** = number read off a *figure image* that I downloaded and looked at (accuracy about +-5 % of axis, or the stated +-). The figure is cited so it can be re-checked.
- **D** = derived by me from T/F numbers plus stated physics (calculation shown).
- **X** = my own measurement on a public audio file (sound example 8 of the KTH lectures). Informal.
- "image only" = the value exists in a figure I could not read numerically.

Source shorthand (all under `https://www.speech.kth.se/music/5_lectures/`):
- **AJ** = Askenfelt & Jansson, `askenflt/<page>.html`
- **HA** = Hall, `hall/<page>.html`
- **CO** = Conklin, `conklin/<page>.html`
- **WE** = Weinreich, `weinreic/<page>.html`
- **WO** = Wogram, `wogram/<page>.html`
- **INTRO** = `introd/introd.html`

Note on Weinreich Fig. 1: the caption says "Eb3 = 311 Hz" but the text says the note is Eb4 and 311 Hz is Eb4; I use Eb4 (k=42). Its trichord had two strings felted, so the curve is one string.

Caveats that apply to nearly every KTH number: these are 1980s measurements on *one or two* instruments. AJ used "one of the grand pianos of the Swedish Radio Company" (model not stated; AJ `coda.html`). HA used a 2 m Grotrian-Steinweg (Hall `compare.html`) and, for the exponent survey, also a new Steinway D, a c. 1800 Muir Wood and a worn CSUS grand (Hall `compare.html`, Fig. 20). WO's impedance/radiation data are from **upright** pianos; only his modal analysis (Figs. 13-17) is a concert grand (2.90 m).

**D-check of the bridge-coupling numbers** (this ties three sources together):
single-string bridge loss alpha = Z_s * G * c / L, with Z_s = string characteristic impedance, G = Re(mobility), c = wave speed.
Take C4: c about 325 m/s (D: 2 L f0 with L about 0.62 m), tension 830 N (average string pull, CO `thecastiron.html`), Z_s = T/c about 2.3 kg/s, and G = 1/1300 s/kg (Steinway D characteristic impedance about 1.3e3 kg/s, arXiv 1210.3948 Fig. 10, see 1c).
That gives alpha about 0.93 1/s = 8.1 dB/s, against Weinreich's measured 8 dB/s (Fig. 1). The agreement supports the impedance level of about 1.3e3 kg/s and the whole picture of the prompt decay being bridge loss of one string. L = 0.62 m is my assumption, not a sourced number.

## 2. New parameters or effects the sources say matter (missing from the spec)

1. **Two polarisations per string.** Double decay exists for a *single* string: vertical polarisation couples strongly to the bridge (prompt), horizontal weakly (aftersound) (Weinreich `motion.html`, Figs. 1-2; also Bank et al., Zenodo 37420 sec. 3, and Renault et al. sec. 5.1 which attributes it to double polarisation plus unison detuning). The spec's prompt/aftersound pair per partial covers this mathematically; but monochords (k<8) also need a nonzero aftersound, and the polarisations radiate through different "antenna patterns" so the level at the microphone depends on the microphone position (Weinreich Fig. 2: the knee region varies with microphone).
2. **Unison locking.** Below about 0.3 Hz mistuning (mid-range) the strings lock, no beats, and the mistuning sets the aftersound level: -41 dB (0.06 Hz), -22 dB (0.22 Hz); at 0.64 Hz beats decay at about the prompt rate (about 8.5 dB/s, peaks -10, -27, -42, -58, -74 dB every 1.85 s, D from Fig. 8). So the aftersound amplitude and mistuning are coupled: a per-key mistuning of 1-2 cents gives the sourced aftersound level; larger mistuning removes the aftersound. Also: damping one of two struck strings makes the level jump up by about 20 dB (WE Fig. 6).
3. **Irregular prompt decay across keys and partials.** Bridge mobility has +-10-15 dB resonance/antiresonance swings (arXiv 1210.5688) and a nodal line of a soundboard mode crossing the bridge near a bass string gives a "very long decay time of the prompt sound" (WO `simulation.html`, Fig. 17: 90 Hz mode of a grand). Add a learnable per-key (and ideally per-partial) log-normal bridge-coupling factor around the mean; an n-independent constant hides the note-to-note unevenness that the sources call typical (INTRO: notes "loud and short while adjacent notes are much softer and longer").
4. **Touch precursor before the tone** (see 1c): 20-30 ms of noise ahead of the hammer contact; the spec's check 8 forbids it.
5. **Hammer/shank resonances**: ripple about 250 Hz (mid), -15 % bass, +80 % treble; backwash about 50 Hz. Part of the knock, more audible above about A4 (CO).
6. **Bridge longitudinal mobility** comparable to the transverse one at 2-3 kHz in the treble (assembled grand, C6 strings): longitudinal string motion couples efficiently into the soundboard at high frequency (arXiv 1210.5688 sec. "Measurements at the bridge", quoting Conklin 1996 and Askenfelt 2006). Relevant to treble "brightness" and phantom partials; Phase 2.
7. **Linearity.** Soundboard/bridge response is linear: linear part at least 50 dB above nonlinear at normal levels (arXiv 1212.3068 abstract); decay independent of blow force 100-800 g (arXiv 1212.2323 sec. 2.1 quoting Hundley et al.). Supports a linear body and velocity-independent decay; only the hammer (and phase-2 tension modulation) is nonlinear.
8. **Hammer condition matters.** Voicing changes the upper spectral limit from about 8 kHz to about 4 kHz (C4 mf) (AJ `voicing.html`, Fig. 19); worn hammers have flat grooves and lose the soft-touch darkness (HA `compare.html`). MAESTRO pianos are concert instruments whose hammers change during a competition year, so B-like per-year "brightness" offsets are justified.
9. **Radiation-loaded damping bump** at 1.2-1.5 kHz of the soundboard (row 1d).
10. **Grand soundboard is not one plate above about 1.1-1.4 kHz**: waves are trapped between ribs; radiation changes; the impedance falls (upright, 3.5x by 2.5 kHz). Body FIR above that band should be a different regime (fewer, localised modes, higher mobility).
